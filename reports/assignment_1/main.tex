This report documents the first two CRISP-DM phases: (1) \textit{Business Understanding} and (2) \textit{Data Understanding}, for an ICU patient care analysis framed around \textit{Regional Medical Center (RMC)}. RMC is described (per the provided project scenario) as a 400-bed academic medical center serving a diverse metropolitan population with a 36-bed intensive care unit (ICU). The motivating need is operational and clinical: ICU leadership seeks evidence-based insight into factors associated with patient outcomes and resource use in order to improve care quality while managing limited ICU capacity.

The analytical work in this report uses the \textit{MIMIC-IV Clinical Database Demo (v2.2)} as the empirical dataset for feasibility assessment and early exploration \cite{PhysioNet-mimic-iv-demo-2.2,Goldberger2000PhysioNet}. The demo dataset contains de-identified electronic health record data for 100 ICU patients and preserves the relational structure of the full MIMIC-IV schema. As a result, the organizational context (RMC) and the data provenance (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center via the MIMIC project) do not represent the same institution. This is intentional and appropriate for the course setting: the RMC scenario defines the decision context and stakeholders, while the MIMIC-IV demo data provides a realistic ICU data environment to validate whether the proposed questions and measurement approach are feasible.

The central analytical theme is ICU resource-use variation, with a primary focus on ICU length of stay (LOS) and related utilization signals. The working hypothesis is that patient characteristics, early treatment patterns, and early physiologic measurements may be associated with materially different LOS distributions. A secondary outcome (return-risk/readmission proxy) remains under feasibility review due to unit-of-analysis requirements and the limited size of the demo cohort. Throughout, the project is framed as a quality-improvement (QI) style decision-support exercise: the goal is to define a defensible decision problem, confirm data feasibility, and establish responsible constraints that will govern downstream preparation and modeling rather than to optimize model performance in isolation.

This report is organized into two main sections. \Cref{sec:business} defines the decision context, stakeholder needs, objectives and success criteria, responsibility analysis, technical translation, and a project plan. \Cref{sec:data_understanding} inventories the available MIMIC-IV demo tables, documents keys and relational structure, summarizes preliminary exploration goals, and identifies data-quality risks that will directly shape subsequent data preparation and modeling choices. Where needed, high-level statements about the MIMIC project and MIMIC-IV scope are supported by the official MIMIC documentation \cite{MIMICAbout}.

\subsubsection*{Organizational/Context Description (Course Scenario: RMC)}
Regional Medical Center (RMC) is modeled (per the provided project scenario) as a 400-bed academic medical center serving a diverse metropolitan population with a 36-bed ICU. The ICU operates under typical academic medical center pressures: high acuity and case mix variation, staffing constraints, and downstream flow dependencies (step-down beds, ward capacity, discharge coordination). The operational mission is to deliver safe, timely critical care while managing limited ICU capacity and reducing avoidable delays in care delivery.


\subsubsection*{Domain and Context Assessment}
ICU care is a high-variance, high-cost environment characterized by heterogeneous patients, complex interventions, and uneven measurement processes. Operationally relevant outcomes (e.g., prolonged ICU stays, step-down delays, and returns to higher levels of care) are influenced by both clinical severity and system factors such as staffing, consult availability, bed management, and documentation practices. As a result, a credible analysis must address not only predictive performance but also interpretability, uncertainty, and responsible communication.

\subsubsection*{Problem Situation (Decision Need)}
The working problem is operationally grounded: ICU leadership benefits from early visibility into which admissions are likely to become prolonged, resource-intensive stays so they can anticipate capacity constraints, coordinate downstream bed flow, and target early interventions or pathway reviews. A related concern is return-risk: some patients may cycle through higher acuity settings or return after discharge, and understanding those patterns requires careful unit-of-analysis definition and conservative interpretation. In the demo cohort, the immediate goal is feasibility validation (what can be measured reliably) rather than asserting hospital-specific effect sizes.

\subsubsection*{Data Science Justification}
ICU electronic health record (EHR) data is inherently multi-table and irregular: diagnoses, labs, charted observations, interventions, admissions, and ICU stays are recorded at different frequencies and with different missingness mechanisms. Data science methods are appropriate because they can (a) consolidate and validate relational structure, (b) characterize missingness and measurement bias, (c) support interpretable stratification of risk and resource use, and (d) evaluate competing definitions of outcomes and cohorts before committing to modeling. Even a SQL-first exploratory phase can materially reduce downstream scope risk by preventing overconfident conclusions from sparse or systematically missing data.

\subsubsection*{Stakeholder Identification}
The primary stakeholder is the ICU Medical Director, who seeks evidence-based insights to improve clinical protocols and care quality and who must judge whether findings are operationally actionable. A second primary stakeholder is Hospital Administration, which is focused on resource utilization, throughput, and quality metrics and requires executive-level summaries with practical implications. Secondary stakeholders include bedside clinicians (who would consume any stratification output), bed management/throughput teams (who care about flow constraints), quality and patient safety (who require conservative claims and documentation), and data governance/security (who care about privacy controls and auditability). Because this is an academic project using a public de-identified dataset, no direct interviews were conducted; stakeholder needs are inferred from standard ICU operational decision points.

\subsubsection*{Primary Objective}
Develop a defensible ICU resource-use analysis foundation that can support decision-relevant stratification of prolonged ICU stay (and related return-risk patterns), using only information that would plausibly be available early in the ICU encounter. In this phase, ``success'' is defined by clear problem framing, computable outcome definitions, validated join structure, and documented constraints that prevent over-claiming.

\subsubsection*{Secondary Objectives}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Stabilize unit-of-analysis:} confirm the appropriate grain (\texttt{stay\_id}, \texttt{hadm\_id}, \texttt{subject\_id}) for each candidate outcome and ensure joins preserve temporal logic.
    \item \textbf{Outcome feasibility checks:} verify that candidate outcomes (prolonged ICU stay; return-risk/readmission proxy) have sufficient support in the demo cohort to enable interpretable analysis.
    \item \textbf{Leakage-aware feature window:} validate that key predictors can be extracted from the first 6--24 hours after ICU admission without using future information (and quantify coverage/missingness).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Legal and Regulatory Considerations}
Although this project uses a de-identified demo dataset rather than a live EHR extract, the intended application domain (ICU decision support) is typically governed by health privacy and security requirements. In a real deployment context, analysis and model outputs could fall under HIPAA-regulated workflows and institutional policies (data access controls, audit logging, and minimum necessary use). For this academic project, the practical constraint is to treat all health data as high-sensitivity by default: avoid unnecessary exposure, avoid re-identification attempts, and document data handling clearly.

\subsubsection*{Privacy and Data Protection}
The repository is designed to prevent accidental data disclosure: raw data files are not committed to version control, and the workflow emphasizes local loading with reproducible scripts rather than distributing extracts. Even with de-identified data, privacy risks are not zero (e.g., linkage risk if combined with external sources). Accordingly, this project does not incorporate external enrichment datasets that could increase re-identification risk.

\subsubsection*{Bias and Fairness Concerns}
Several bias sources are plausible in ICU data and are relevant even at the feasibility stage:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Representation bias:} a small demo cohort may underrepresent key subpopulations, making subgroup comparisons unstable or misleading.
    \item \textbf{Measurement bias:} sicker patients are often measured more frequently; missingness can encode workflow and acuity rather than ``absence of signal.''
    \item \textbf{Proxy risk:} variables correlated with socioeconomic factors or access patterns may unintentionally act as proxies, complicating interpretation.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Stakeholder Impact Analysis}
\textbf{Potential benefits} include improved clarity on what can be signaled early, more transparent resource-use stratification, and better scoping of future analysis work. \textbf{Potential harms} include overgeneralizing from small counts, implicitly stigmatizing cohorts if segmentation is poorly defined, or creating unjustified confidence in patterns driven by documentation practices rather than clinical reality.

\subsubsection*{Mitigation Strategies}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Feasibility gates:} refuse to lock outcomes/cohorts that yield too few cases for stable interpretation.
    \item \textbf{Conservative claims:} explicitly separate association from causation and report uncertainty.
    \item \textbf{Missingness characterization:} quantify missingness and discuss whether it appears systematic.
    \item \textbf{Cohort discipline:} start with coarse groupings and only refine if sample sizes support it.
    \item \textbf{Transparency artifacts:} maintain a decision log (assumptions, pivots, thresholds) so stakeholder interpretation is properly scoped.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Technical Problem Framing}
The primary technical framing is a supervised learning problem focused on early risk stratification:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Primary:} binary classification to predict prolonged ICU stay risk at a defined prediction time (e.g., 24 hours after ICU admission).
    \item \textbf{Secondary (contingent on feasibility):} admission-level return-risk/readmission proxy as binary classification.
\end{itemize}
Alternative framings (e.g., LOS regression or time-to-event modeling) remain possible but are deferred until the feasibility profile confirms that outcome definitions and sample support are stable.

\subsubsection*{Data Science Objectives}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Produce a reproducible cohort/outcome dataset at the selected grain, with leakage-aware early-window features.
    \item Establish baseline descriptive performance (simple interpretable models) before considering more complex methods.
    \item Quantify uncertainty and stability (sensitivity to cohort definitions and thresholds) as part of evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Technical Success Criteria (Provisional)}
Given the small demo cohort, technical targets emphasize interpretability and stability over headline performance:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \textbf{Minimum viability:} baseline model exceeds naive prevalence prediction on discrimination metrics (e.g., AUC/PR-AUC) \emph{and} shows reasonable calibration.
    \item \textbf{Operational usefulness:} the model can identify a higher-risk subset (e.g., top 20\% risk) with materially higher observed prolonged-stay rate than baseline (TBD after feasibility).
    \item \textbf{Transparency:} feature logic and timing constraints are documented; no post-outcome variables included.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Analytical Approach Overview}
The planned approach is interpretability-first:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item Confirm grain and joins; define outcomes with explicit gates.
    \item Extract early-window candidate predictors (6--24h) with coverage profiling.
    \item Start with simple models (e.g., logistic regression) and compare against a baseline.
    \item Evaluate using cross-validation where feasible, with emphasis on stability and uncertainty reporting.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Primary Dataset Description}
Primary dataset: the MIMIC-IV Clinical Database Demo (v2.2), an open-access subset of the MIMIC-IV clinical database \cite{PhysioNet-mimic-iv-demo-2.2,Goldberger2000PhysioNet}. The demo bundle is distributed as CSV files organized into \texttt{hosp/} and \texttt{icu/} modules, plus a cohort mapping file (\texttt{demo\_subject\_id.csv}) that defines the included population (100 distinct \texttt{subject\_id}s). In this project workspace, the raw files are stored under \texttt{data/raw/ICU\_Patients/data/raw}.

For reproducibility, a local file inventory (names, sizes, and modified timestamps) and checksums were captured after download. All clinical CSV files in the bundle share an upstream modified timestamp of \textbf{2023-01-09 08:43} (per local metadata), with accompanying documentation files (\texttt{README.txt}, \texttt{LICENSE.txt}, and \texttt{SHA256SUMS.txt}). In addition to the vendor-provided checksum file (\texttt{SHA256SUMS.txt}), a local checksum manifest (\texttt{SHA256SUMS\_local\_generated.txt}, generated \textbf{2026-02-15}) was produced to lock the exact file versions used in this analysis.

To support exploratory SQL profiling and reproducible transforms, the CSVs were loaded into a local PostgreSQL database named \texttt{mimic\_demo}. The resulting database footprint is approximately \textbf{185 MB}.
\subsubsection*{Additional Data Sources}
No supplementary datasets are included in scope for this phase. This is a deliberate privacy and scope-control choice: external enrichment could introduce linkage risk and distract from feasibility validation. Supporting materials include dataset documentation and course CRISP-DM guidance used for interpretation and planning.
Key data characteristics relevant to analysis include patient-level date shifting that preserves within-patient intervals, synthetic identifiers, shifted representation of ages over 89 years, exclusion of free-text clinical notes, and the possibility of empty or sparse tables due to the 100-patient subset \cite{PhysioNet-mimic-iv-demo-2.2}.

\subsubsection*{Data Collection Methodology}

\subsubsection*{Dataset Structure and Relationships}
The dataset is relational and supports multiple grains. The working model assumes:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item \texttt{patients} (one \texttt{subject\_id}) has many \texttt{admissions} (\texttt{hadm\_id}).
    \item \texttt{admissions} may contain one or more ICU stays (\texttt{stay\_id}) via \texttt{icustays}.
    \item Event tables (e.g., labs, charted observations) typically link to \texttt{hadm\_id} and/or \texttt{stay\_id} and may be many-to-one relative to the chosen grain.
\end{itemize}
Join routes and referential consistency are validated in the SQL inventory pass and summarized in \Cref{subsec:data_quality}.

\subsubsection*{Temporal Coverage and Granularity (Constraint)}
Within-encounter interval timing is usable for tasks like early-window feature extraction and LOS computation. However, de-identification date shifting limits analyses that rely on true calendar alignment across patients (e.g., seasonality, real-world census over dates). This constraint directly informs the decision to prioritize within-stay temporal structure rather than population-wide calendar trends.

\subsubsection*{Data Volume and Scale}
The demo cohort is small (approximately $N \approx 100$ patients). Full record counts per table and event density summaries are reported once the inventory pass is completed (TBD placeholders in \Cref{tab:data_inventory} will be replaced with measured values).

\subsubsection*{Categorical Variable Encoding (Cohort Strategy)}
Cohorts will be constructed using clinically meaningful but coarse groupings to avoid over-fragmentation. The initial approach is ICD-informed grouping supplemented by admission/service context where available. Cohort definitions will be versioned and reported with counts per cohort to prevent interpretive drift.

Several data characteristics follow directly from de-identification and sampling in the demo dataset: identifiers are synthetic, dates are shifted by patient-specific offsets while preserving intervals, ages above 89 are transformed for privacy, free-text notes are excluded, and some clinical event tables may be sparse or empty because only 100 patients are included \cite{PhysioNet-mimic-iv-demo-2.2}. \subsubsection*{Completeness Analysis}
Completeness will be quantified for: (a) key identifiers and timestamps needed for LOS and early windows, and (b) core candidate predictors (vitals/labs) within the first 6--24 hours. Particular attention is paid to whether missingness appears systematic (workflow/acuity-driven) rather than random.

\subsubsection*{Accuracy Assessment}
Accuracy checks will flag implausible values (outliers), inconsistent timestamps (e.g., negative durations), and duplicate or conflicting records where applicable. Given the dataset context, these checks are framed as data integrity validation rather than clinical chart audit.

\subsubsection*{Consistency Evaluation}
Consistency checks focus on referential integrity and grain alignment across \texttt{subject\_id}, \texttt{hadm\_id}, and \texttt{stay\_id}, including whether event tables reliably map to the selected modeling grain without unintended duplication inflation.

\subsubsection*{Timeliness and Currency}
The demo dataset is not intended for real-time operational monitoring. Accordingly, the analysis focuses on methodological feasibility and decision framing rather than benchmarking current-year ICU performance.

\subsubsection*{Relevance and Coverage}
Coverage risk is moderate-to-high due to demo cohort size and potential subgroup imbalance. This directly constrains cohort granularity and determines whether readmission/return-risk analyses remain viable.

\subsubsection*{Data Quality Impact Assessment}
Data quality will shape scope more than algorithm choice in this project stage. If outcome counts are too small or early-window coverage is too sparse, the plan explicitly pivots to fewer cohorts and more descriptive analysis rather than forcing a weak predictive model.
